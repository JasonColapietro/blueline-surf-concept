Surf report, rentals and lessons: the pages a coastal Florida shop actually needs

Five pages, each answering one question a customer asks before they ever call. A practical plan from Suede AI in West Palm Beach.

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI, West Palm Beach, Florida

Most coastal shop websites in Palm Beach County have the same problem: one long homepage trying to be a surf report, a price list, a lesson calendar and a store at once. Google and AI answer engines cannot tell what the page is about, and neither can the person searching "paddleboard rental Jupiter" from a boat ramp.

This is the page plan Jason Colapietro uses at Suede AI when scoping a surf, paddle or watersports site in Jupiter, Juno Beach or Tequesta. It grew out of the Blueline concept case study, an independent concept not affiliated with the real shop, and it applies to any shop on the Treasure Coast or the Palm Beaches.

The rule: one page, one question

Each page below exists because a customer asks a specific question. Suede AI writes that question at the top of every page brief. If a page cannot state its question in one sentence, it probably should not exist. That rule is what lets a search engine or an AI model quote the page directly instead of guessing.

1. The surf report page

Question it answers: "Is it worth going out right now, and when is the next good window?"

  • Data: swell height, period and direction, wind speed and direction, water temperature, and tide times. Open-Meteo's marine and forecast models are free and solid. For tides on this stretch of coast, NOAA CO-OPS publishes predictions for station 8722670 at Lake Worth Pier.
  • Labeling: print the source and the update time next to the numbers. Call model data a forecast, not a reading.
  • Human note: one or two sentences from staff each morning ("knee-high and clean at Carlin early, onshore by ten") beats any widget. That line is also what AI answers tend to quote.
  • Do not: embed a camera feed you do not have rights to, or show stale numbers without a timestamp.

2. The rentals page

Question it answers: "What can I rent, what does it cost, and how do I get it to the water?"

  • Put rates in an HTML table, not a JPEG of the counter sign. Hourly and full-day columns are enough for most shops.
  • Spell out what is included (leash, paddle, rack straps, life vest) and what is not.
  • If you deliver, name the delivery zone by place: the Jupiter Inlet sandbar, Dubois Park, specific beach accesses.
  • State the weather and refund policy above the booking button, in one sentence.

3. The lessons page

Question it answers: "Can I or my kid learn here, with whom, where and when?"

  • List lesson types with age ranges, group sizes, duration and location (Carlin Park and the Juno Pier are the obvious ones in Jupiter).
  • Show real instructors with real certifications. If an instructor holds an ISA or lifeguard credential, say which one. If they do not, do not imply it. The concept uses placeholder instructors for exactly this reason.
  • Show next available slots and the price before checkout.

4. The visit page

Question it answers: "Where are you, when are you open, and where do I park?"

Address, phone and hours as text that matches your Google Business Profile character for character. Add parking and loading notes. This page is also where LocalBusiness structured data belongs, filled with your own verified details. Inconsistent business data across your site and listings is one of the most common reasons a local shop gets left out of AI answers.

5. Notes or a small journal

Question it answers: "Do these people know this water?"

Three to six short articles a year is enough: a sandbar guide, a first paddleboard buying guide, a hurricane season gear checklist. Specific local knowledge is what AI systems cite, and it is what Jason Colapietro and the Suede team mean by content that earns its place.

How AI search reads these pages

Answer engines pull short, self-contained passages. The pages that get quoted are usually the ones that answer first. Pages built on the one-question rule give them clean passages to lift. A few habits help:

  • Open each page with a two-sentence direct answer, then the detail.
  • Use the place names locals use, consistently, across every page.
  • Keep prices, hours and policies as text, never only inside images or PDFs.
  • Link related pages to each other with descriptive anchors ("book a Carlin Park lesson," not "click here").

Common mistakes we see in Palm Beach County

  • Booking only through a third-party widget with no page content around it. The widget is fine; the empty page is the problem.
  • Seasonal pages that vanish. Keep the summer camp page live year-round and update the dates.
  • Reviews pasted as text with no source. Link to where they live, or leave them out.
  • No mobile thumb test. Most of these searches happen in a parking lot. If the rates table needs pinch-zoom, rebuild it.
  • Copying a competitor's page structure. Two Jupiter shops with near-identical rental pages give an answer engine no reason to quote either one.
