Case study: a Jupiter surf and paddle shop website concept by Suede AI

A surf shop site has one job at sunrise: tell a surfer whether to load the car. Here is how this concept answers that, and where it stops being real.

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI, West Palm Beach, Florida

At 6:40 a.m. a surf shop website has exactly one job: tell someone standing in a Jupiter kitchen whether the Juno Pier is worth the drive. Most shop sites answer with a hero carousel and a newsletter popup. This concept, designed by Jason Colapietro at Suede AI in West Palm Beach, Florida, answers with swell height, wind, water temperature and the next tide before you scroll an inch.

Read this first. Blueline Surf & Paddle Co. is a real Jupiter shop, and this is an independent, uncommissioned design concept. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by the business. Product names, prices, ratings, reviews and staff on the concept homepage are illustrative placeholders, and the homepage is deliberately kept out of search indexes for that reason.

What the concept set out to prove

Jason Colapietro wanted to test one idea: a coastal shop site should be organized around the ocean, not around the catalog. Shoppers in Palm Beach County do not arrive asking for "products." They arrive asking whether it is glassy, whether a paddleboard is available by 8 a.m., and whether a nine-year-old can take a lesson on Saturday. The Suede brief for the build was three sentences long: conditions first, booking second, catalog third.

That order shows up in the page itself. The live conditions panel overlaps the hero. Categories come next, then the board rack, then rentals, lessons, the social feed and the footer with a schematic map of the Jupiter Inlet.

Conditions first: the live Juno Pier panel

The panel pulls current wave height, swell period, direction and sea surface temperature from the Open-Meteo marine model, wind, air temperature and UV from the Open-Meteo forecast model, and high and low tide predictions from NOAA CO-OPS station 8722670 at Lake Worth Pier. It refreshes every 15 minutes and prints its sources in plain text under the numbers.

Two decisions matter more than the code:

  • Say where the numbers come from. Model output is not a buoy reading, and a surfer who gets skunked by a forecast should be able to see why. The source line is part of the design, not a footnote.
  • Fail honestly. When the feed cannot load, the panel switches to sample values and says so in words. A blank widget reads as broken; fake numbers presented as live would be worse.

The tradeoff is real, and Jason Colapietro says so up front: a free model feed is good enough for a concept and for most small shops, but a production build for a busy shop might add a paid buoy or camera partner. That is a budget conversation, not a design one.

Booking that respects the inlet

Rentals are presented as a plain rate table with hourly and full-day columns, written as HTML text rather than a flyer image, so search engines, screen readers and AI answer engines can all read it. The lesson module lets a visitor pick private, group or groms, choose a time slot, and see a running total before committing. The reschedule policy ("blown out? we'll paddle instead") sits right under the button because that is the question people ask at the counter.

Every number in those modules is a placeholder. A real build would pull rates and availability from whatever booking system the shop already runs, so staff never maintain prices in two places.

Local signals a Palm Beach County search can read

Jupiter is a small market with very specific geography, and the concept leans into it. The copy names Carlin Park, the Juno Pier, Dubois Park, the Jupiter Inlet sandbar, Tequesta and Palm Beach Gardens because those are the words locals type. The footer map is an inline SVG drawn for the page, not an embedded map frame, so it loads instantly and carries text labels a crawler can parse.

One honest weakness: the concept ships as a single HTML file with its photography embedded, which puts it around 1.5 MB. That made it easy to present and share. A production version would split images into compressed files and lazy-load everything below the fold. What a real build would add

If a shop like this commissioned the work, Jason Colapietro and the Suede team would change four things:

  1. Real reviews only. Pull verified reviews from the shop's Google Business Profile, with dates and links. No invented quotes, no star counts that cannot be traced.
  2. Owned business data. LocalBusiness structured data using the shop's own address, phone and hours, matched exactly to its listings.
  3. Rentals and lessons as their own pages, each answering one question well. We wrote about that in the pages a coastal Florida shop actually needs.
  4. A measurement plan that tracks booked lessons and rentals, plus a monthly check of how the shop appears in AI answers for "surf lessons Jupiter" and similar prompts.
