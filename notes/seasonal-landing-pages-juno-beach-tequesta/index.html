Seasonal landing pages for a coastal shop: snowbird season, summer camps and storm closures (Juno Beach and Tequesta)

Three pages that stay live all year, one per season that changes what customers ask. A plan from Suede AI using the Blueline concept.

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI, West Palm Beach, Florida

A surf and paddle shop in Jupiter runs three different businesses each year. From roughly November through April it serves seasonal residents and visitors who want calm-water paddles and gear; in June and July it runs kids' camps; and between June 1 and November 30 it lives with hurricane season, when a single storm can close the shop, the beaches and the rental fleet for days.

Most shop websites handle all three with one banner at the top of the homepage. This note lays out a better pattern: a small set of seasonal landing pages that stay live all year, tuned to real audiences in Juno Beach and Tequesta, written by Jason Colapietro of Suede AI using the Blueline concept (an independent concept, not affiliated with the real shop) as the worked example.

Why seasonal pages, not seasonal banners

A banner disappears when the season ends, and so does everything search engines and AI answer engines learned from it. A dedicated page builds history. When someone asks an AI assistant "kids surf camp near Tequesta this summer," the answer is usually drawn from a page that has existed for more than one summer and has a clear date on it, not from a homepage banner that went up last week.

The rule Suede uses: if a season changes what customers ask, it gets its own URL, and that URL never gets deleted. Dates and prices change each year; the address stays.

Page 1: the winter season page

Audience: seasonal residents and visitors staying in Juno Beach condos and along US-1, including paddlers who know the sport but not this stretch of water.

  • Lead with what changes in winter. Cooler water, stronger cold-front winds and bigger north swells are typical, so say what that means for rentals and lessons rather than promising flat water.
  • Offer what this audience actually books: weekly or monthly rentals, early-morning calm-water paddles on the Intracoastal, and gear storage if you offer it.
  • Write for arrival week. A short "first week here" section covering launch spots, parking and where to rinse gear answers the questions seasonal visitors ask the counter.

This is a real audience page, not a town keyword page. The Juno Beach angle shows up as useful detail (where people stay, which launch points are close), not as the town name repeated in every heading.

Page 2: the summer camp page

Audience: parents in Tequesta, Jupiter and Palm Beach Gardens planning summer weeks, often months ahead.

  • Keep this page live all year. Parents start comparing camps in late winter, and a page that returns a 404 in February loses them.
  • Put the facts in text and tables: age range, weekly dates, daily hours, drop-off spot, student-to-instructor ratio, price and what is included. Show instructor credentials only if they are real and current.
  • Answer the safety questions directly: who is in the water with the kids, what happens on a lightning afternoon, and how parents are contacted.
  • When this year's dates are not set yet, say so with a month ("2027 dates post in February") and offer a notify-me signup.

On the concept homepage, one instructor card lists a "Groms program" for young surfers. A real build would turn that into this page, with a dated "last updated" line under the heading.

Page 3: the storm status page

Audience: everyone with a booking, a rental out, or a question about whether you are open after a storm.

This page is the one most coastal shops do not have, and it is the one people search for most urgently. Build it before June, link it from the header during hurricane season, and leave it in place year-round with a calm "No current closures" state.

  • A status line at the top with a date and time: open, closed, or reopening on a named day.
  • The booking policy for storms in plain words: lessons and rentals during a closure move or refund automatically, and how customers hear about it.
  • Links to official sources for beach and park closures, such as Palm Beach County and the National Hurricane Center, instead of restating their orders.
  • A light page. After a storm, people check on weak cell signal. Plain text loads; a heavy page with video does not.

A surf shop's storm page is simpler, but the habits carry over.

Juno Beach and Tequesta without doorway pages

It is tempting to clone each seasonal page per town: "summer camp Tequesta," "summer camp Juno Beach," "summer camp Jupiter." Search engines treat near-identical town pages as doorway pages, and AI answer engines have no reason to quote any of them. Suede does the opposite. One page per season, with real local detail where it belongs: the Juno Beach launch points on the winter page, Tequesta drop-off and carpool notes on the camp page, and the beach access and park names that matter after a storm.

That detail is also what gets cited. A sentence written in the form "camp meets at [named beach access] at [start time]; drop-off is in [named lot]" is specific, checkable and quotable, as long as every bracket is filled with the shop's real details.

The technical checklist

  • A stable URL per season, with the year in the content and the "last updated" date, never in the slug.
  • A visible date on every page plus dateModified in the page's structured data. Use Event markup only for real camp sessions with real dates.
  • Internal links from the rentals, lessons and visit pages to the matching seasonal page.
  • Prices, hours and policies as text, never only in a flyer image.

Before the next season turns, write down the three questions customers ask most in that season and check whether any page on your site answers them in text. If none does, that is your first seasonal page.
